Runaway Protection · Plain-Language Companion

How Solwyn catches a runaway AI agent — and stops it

AI agents sometimes get stuck in loops. They call the AI over and over, doing no useful work, and every call costs money. This page explains, in plain words, the plan to spot those loops, shut them down with one click, and show you the money you didn't lose.

01

The problem, in two true stories

These are real incidents from the industry. Nobody noticed until the bill arrived. Spending caps exist, but a cap alone just means the run dies at the limit. Nothing on the market stops a loop while it is running. That is the gap this plan claims.

$47,000
Two agents talked to each other for 11 days
Each reply fed the next one. The conversation snowballed. Nobody was watching.
$4,200
One agent retried the same failing call for 63 hours
Same request, over and over, all weekend. Every retry cost money.
02

The plan has three parts

See it. Stop it. Prove it. Each part builds on machinery Solwyn already shipped, which is why most of this is assembly, not invention.

Part 1 · Detect

See the loop as it starts

The Solwyn software that sits inside your app watches the shape of the calls: how big, how often, how fast they grow. Loops have shapes. Retries look identical. Snowballs keep growing.

Like a credit card company: it flags five identical charges in a minute without reading what you bought.
Part 2 · Terminate

Kill it with one click

A "Kill run" button in your dashboard. The stop order rides back on messages the software already exchanges with Solwyn, so the run halts within one call or a few seconds. No new plumbing, no waiting.

Like a circuit breaker: flip it once, the power stays off, even if the toaster keeps asking.
Part 3 · Prove

Show the money you saved

Every blocked call gets a receipt: what was blocked, who blocked it, and roughly what it would have cost. The receipts add up to one number: what the cap saved you.

Like an itemized statement: not just "we stopped it," but "here's each thing we stopped."
03

It never reads your data

This is the part competitors can't copy. To find loops, they read your prompts on their servers. Solwyn finds loops using only numbers, inside your own app, and sends out only the verdict. An automated test suite enforces this on every code change.

What it looks at
What it never sees
  • How big each call is a size estimate, just a number
  • How often calls happen timestamps and counts
  • Which run they belong to an ID label, like a ticket number
  • Which model was asked a name, like "gpt-5"
  • Your prompts the words you send the AI
  • The AI's answers
  • Your documents or user data
  • Anything typed by your customers
04

Three tripwires ship first

Each tripwire matches one known loop shape. All numbers below are the starting defaults and can be tuned.

Same size, on repeat

Five calls of nearly identical size to the same model within a minute. That's the shape of a stuck retry — the $4,200 story.

default: 5 near-twins in 60 seconds
can auto-stop (if you opt in)

The snowball

Eight calls in a row, each bigger than the last, growing fast with no human-speed pauses. That's two agents feeding each other — the $47,000 story.

default: 8 growing calls, 3× total growth
can auto-stop (if you opt in)

The sudden burst

Call rate jumps to triple the recent pace. Sometimes a loop — but sometimes just a busy job doing honest work in parallel.

default: 30+ calls/min and 3× the prior minute
warns only, never stops
The safety posture: warn first. Out of the box, tripwires only raise a flag — in your logs and on your dashboard. Nothing gets stopped automatically unless you turn that on. And the burst tripwire can never stop anything, because busy-but-honest work trips it too often. You watch the flags, tune the numbers, and only then decide if you want auto-stop.
05

What happens when you click "Kill run"

The whole point of the design: the stop order needs no new connection to your app. It hitches a ride on check-ins that already happen.

1
Your app
An agent run starts looping. The tripwire flags it.
2
Your dashboard
Within seconds, the run shows a Flagged badge. You look, and it does look wrong.
3
Your dashboard
You click Kill run and confirm. Solwyn's servers mark that run as dead.
4
Your app
The very next time the run checks in — before its next call, or at its regular allowance renewal a few seconds later — the stop order comes back with the answer. The run halts. Even a half-finished streaming reply stops at its next chunk.
5
Your dashboard
Every call the dead run tries afterward is blocked and gets a receipt. The "saved you" number grows with each one.

The stop is sticky. Your app remembers the kill locally, so even if Solwyn's own servers go down right after, the dead run stays dead. And your code hears a clear, distinct error — "this run was terminated" — not a vague failure it might retry.

A pleasant surprise: much of this already exists

Before planning, every claim was checked against the real code — on both the app side and the server side. Two claims in the original pitch turned out to be wrong in a good way.

Already built & running
  • Blocked calls already send a report event the pitch said they left no trace — they do
  • The "saved you" dollar total already exists on the server and the dashboard already shows a "~$X saved" note
  • Each run already shows its lifetime blocked cost in the dashboard
  • The message channel a stop order needs is already proven a different server order already rides it today
  • The "stay blocked even during outages" memory already works per run
What this plan adds
  • The three tripwires nothing watches call shapes today
  • The kill order, the Kill button, and the clear "terminated" error no kill concept exists anywhere yet
  • Receipts that say who blocked a call and why today's reports are anonymous
  • Receipts that survive outages today, reports during an outage can get lost — exactly when blocking happens most
  • Mid-stream stopping today a long reply can't be interrupted
07

The build order

Eleven work packages across three codebases — the app-side software, the server, and the dashboard — grouped into three phases. Each package is one reviewable chunk with its own tests.

Phase 1App-side only · 3 packages

Tripwires, local kill switch, stream stopping

Everything that works without touching the server: the three tripwires (warn mode), the machinery to stop a run and raise the clear error, and mid-stream stopping. Opt-in auto-stop works here too.

Payoff: a working local demo — loop detected, loop killed, no server changes needed
Phase 2Server + dashboard · 5 packages

The Kill button, wired end to end

The server learns to store a kill, attach the stop order to check-ins, and accept the richer receipts. The dashboard gets the Flagged badge and the Kill button. Server changes always ship first so old app versions never break.

Payoff: the flagship demo — "we saw the loop, you killed it from the dashboard, here's the receipt"
Phase 3Polish · 3 packages

Receipts everywhere, outage-proof

Each blocked call's receipt appears in the cost views with its reason. The saved-you number gains an "up to" range. Receipts that can't be delivered during an outage get bundled into tallies and sent when the connection returns, instead of being lost.

Payoff: the "what the cap saved you" story is complete and trustworthy
08

Two decisions that need you

Everything else in the plan is settled. These two are product judgment calls, and building starts after you confirm them.

Decision 1

Should tripwires only warn by default?

The plan says yes: out of the box, flags only. Auto-stop is something a customer turns on after watching the flags for a while. The alternative is auto-stop from day one, which catches loops faster but risks killing honest work and losing trust.

Recommendation: warn first. A false alarm that warns costs nothing. A false alarm that kills costs a customer.
Decision 2

Should a kill beat "alert-only" mode?

Some projects run Solwyn in a gentle mode that warns about overspending but never blocks. If you click Kill on such a project, should the run still stop? The plan says yes: a human pressing the button should always win.

Recommendation: the button always wins. Clicking Kill is an explicit order, not a budget policy.
09

What could go wrong, and the answer for each

The full plan lists more; these are the four worth knowing about.

False alarms on honest work

A job that fans out fifty similar tasks looks a bit like a retry loop. Answer: warn-first defaults, tight matching rules, and the noisy burst tripwire can never kill. Every flag is logged so the numbers can be tuned before anyone enables auto-stop.

A kill that silently fails

The worst outcome: you click Kill, believe it worked, and the run keeps spending. Answer: the stop-order code is built to fail loudly. If it can't attach the order, the whole check-in errors — it never quietly drops the kill.

Receipts lost during outages

Blocking happens most when Solwyn's own servers are unreachable — exactly when reports can't be delivered. Answer: undeliverable receipts fold into a small tally kept in memory and are sent when the connection returns.

A loop split across two apps

If two separate app processes share one run, each sees only half the calls and might not trip a wire alone. Answer: both halves' flags still land in the dashboard, where you see the whole picture and can kill it. Fully automatic cross-app detection is future work, on purpose.

Six words the plan uses a lot

Open the plain-word glossary
run
One job an agent is doing, with its own ID. Like one ticket at a help desk. Kills and receipts are per run.
the SDK
The Solwyn software that lives inside your app and wraps your AI calls. This is where the tripwires run.
check-in
The quick "am I still within budget?" question your app asks Solwyn around its AI calls. Stop orders ride back on the answers.
lease
A prepaid spending allowance for a busy run, so it doesn't have to ask before every single call. Leases renew every few seconds — another ride for the stop order.
sticky denial
Once a run is refused, your app remembers it locally. Even if Solwyn goes offline, the refused run stays refused.
receipt
The record a blocked call leaves behind: what was blocked, who blocked it, why, and roughly what it would have cost.
