01

How the fence works today

Think of the AI provider as a building full of doors. Each door is one thing the service can do: chat, make images, transcribe audio. Some doors cost money every time you open them. Solwyn stands at the doors it knows. Today there are four kinds of doors.

Watched doors

metered

Every use is checked against the budget first, then counted. This is the product working as promised.

Chat, embeddings, images, transcription, speech, video.

Noted doors

warn once

Solwyn says one sentence in the logs, once, ever. Then the door stays open forever, uncounted.

Exactly one door is on this list today: audio translation.

Locked doors

blocked

Solwyn refuses to open them, because their cost can't be measured without reading your private content. You get a clear error instead.

Three Amazon Bedrock doors, like invoke_model.

Silent doors

the problem

Every door not on a list is wide open with no note, no count, no alarm. And every NEW door a provider ships starts life in this pile.

This is the hole in the fence.
Silent pass-through would be a budget bypass.
The codebase's own words, explaining why the Bedrock doors are locked. The same logic, applied everywhere, is this whole plan.
02

What we found behind the silent doors

While writing the plan, we walked the building and checked every door against the real provider software. The silent pile is not full of harmless closets. It holds some of the most expensive doors in the building.

responses OpenAI's newest flagship API. The one OpenAI now tells everyone to build on. It spends money on every call. Solwyn does not watch it, does not note it, does not count it. A well-known competitor product had this exact blind spot and it became a public bug report.
chat.completions.parse A helper inside the watched chat room. Same cost as chat, but it slips around the meter through a side hatch.
messages.stream · messages.batches Anthropic's streaming helper and its bulk-processing service. Both spend money. Both silent.
batches · fine_tuning · realtime · evals … A dozen more OpenAI doors that bill real dollars: bulk jobs, model training, live voice, evaluations. All silent today.
invoke_model_with_bidirectional_stream The clincher. Amazon shipped a new sibling of the very doors Solwyn locks, after the locks were installed. The new sibling? Silent. This proves the point: lists rot, and new doors always start unwatched.
03

The idea we borrowed from aviation

The Minimum Equipment List

Before any airliner takes off, every piece of equipment must be on a list that says one of two things: it works, or it's broken and here is why flying is still safe anyway. There is no third category. Nothing flies unlisted, and "nobody ever wrote it down" is not an answer.

This plan applies that rule to spending: every door gets a label, and "unlabeled" becomes a loud state instead of a silent one.

04

The fix, in three pieces

a.

The list — a label for every door

One maintained table in the code. No guessing from names. If it's not on the list, that itself is a label.
metered

Counted. Budget checked before, cost recorded after.

costs money, uncounted

Known to bill, not yet watched. The dial below decides what happens here.

blocked

Refused outright. Cost is unmeasurable without reading private content.

housekeeping

Free stuff. Listing models, checking files. Passes through quietly, always.

plumbing

Not a door at all. Settings and internals of the client itself. Always fine.

unknown

Never seen it before. Treated as if it costs money, until someone labels it. This is the MEL rule.

b.

The dial — you choose how strict

One setting, on_unmetered, with three positions. It only governs the risky labels: "costs money, uncounted" and "unknown."
default

warn

Let it through, but say so. One log line per door, per app run. Today's silence is gone; nothing breaks.

raise

Strict mode. Refuse the door with a clear error, unless you've signed off on it by name. For teams where an untracked dollar is worse than a stopped program.

allow

Let it through quietly. An explicit "I know, stop telling me." The door still shows up on the receipt below.

c.

The receipt — proof you can pin

One function, solwyn.coverage(client), prints the full door list with labels. No network call, nothing sent anywhere.
a customer's test file · runs on every code change
# "Here is every uncounted door I accept. Nothing else."
report = solwyn.coverage(client)
report.expect(
    untracked=["audio.translations", "beta", "responses"],
    unknown=[],   # zero surprise doors tolerated
)

This is the drift alarm. The day a provider ships a new door, or a Solwyn update starts (or stops) watching one, this check fails the customer's build with a message naming the exact door. Coverage rot stops being silent decay and becomes a red light. The same list is exported as a data file so the future TypeScript SDK uses identical labels.

05

Why "warn" stays the default

This was the one real product judgment in the plan. Should the strict setting be the default? We weighed it and said no. Here is the scale.

If strict were the default…

  • Provider updates would break customers. When OpenAI ships a harmless new feature, it's "unknown" to Solwyn until we label it. Strict-by-default turns that into a crash, in production, for touching something that spends nothing.
  • Frameworks poke at clients constantly. Popular AI libraries check "does this client have X?" dozens of times. Strict-by-default booby-traps those checks.
  • Solwyn's promise is "drop-in." A wrapper that randomly breaks after a routine update is not drop-in.

What "warn" plus the receipt gives you

  • The silent category is abolished either way. Every money door speaks up at least once. That's the actual fix.
  • Strict teams get strict. One setting flips it, and the error message tells you the exact sign-off to paste.
  • The receipt catches drift without crashing anything. The build fails, in CI, before production ever sees a new door.
The call: default stays warn. Strict mode (raise) is one line away, and the README will recommend strict + a pinned receipt for anyone whose spending rules matter more than uptime. Worth noting: the famous scary example, "listing your files would crash by default," was never real. File listing is labeled housekeeping and passes under every setting.
06

How it ships

Three pull requests, each one reviewable and tested on its own. Nothing lands half-wired.

PR 1 · The list

Every door gets a label; the silent category dies

The label table becomes real code, replacing two scattered half-lists. All 20+ silent money doors start warning. A new test builds real provider clients and fails our own build if any door is unlabeled, so provider updates force us to keep the list current.

PR 2 · The dial

Strict mode arrives

The on_unmetered setting, the sign-off list, and the clear refusal error. Tests prove a brand-new unknown door on a fake client gets caught in strict mode, and that the default behaves exactly like today, just louder.

PR 3 · The receipt

Coverage report + the CI pin

solwyn.coverage(), the expect() check that fails on drift in either direction, and the README rewrite. The docs stop listing these holes as "known limitations" and start explaining the fence.

07

The two judgment calls — decided

Everything else was a routine judgment. These two shaped the product. Both were reviewed and approved on Aug 7, 2026.

1

Default = warn, not strict approved

The whole of section 05. Warn is the default. The fence still closes; strict is opt-in.

The road not taken: strict-by-default would let routine provider updates crash customer apps on harmless doors until a label update ships. Rejected.

2

Strict errors bend for "just checking" approved

In strict mode, when a library merely asks whether a door exists, the answer is a polite "no such door." When code actually walks through it, it gets the loud refusal. This keeps popular frameworks from crashing on their routine checks.

The accepted cost: sloppy code that catches all "no such door" answers could muffle the refusal and quietly fall back. The receipt still exposes the door in CI, which is why this trade is acceptable.

08

The fine print

Deliberately out of scope, and the honest risk list. Click to expand.

What this plan deliberately does not do
  • It doesn't start counting the new doors. Making responses or batch jobs fully metered is future work. This plan makes the holes loud and refusable, and hands us a ranked to-do list.
  • It doesn't fix the solwyn_tags quirk (a labeling option that errors on unwatched doors). Fixing it would mean disguising every passed-through object, which breaks the "drop-in, untouched" promise. Deferred, with reasons written down.
  • No server or dashboard changes. The receipt is computed locally and sends nothing anywhere.
  • Privacy unchanged: the labels are door names only. No prompts, no responses, ever.
